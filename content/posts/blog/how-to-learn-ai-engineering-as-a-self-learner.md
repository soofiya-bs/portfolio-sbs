There has never been more free material for learning AI — and it has never been easier to get lost in it. Most self-learners don't fail from a lack of resources. They fail from a lack of **structure**: jumping between tutorials, starting courses they never finish, and never quite knowing if they're making progress.

Here's an approach that works.

## Know what "applied AI engineer" means

An applied AI engineer **builds useful products with AI models** — they don't usually invent new models. The job looks like:

- Building backends and APIs that use LLMs safely
- Connecting models to real data with RAG and search
- Building agents and automations for real workflows
- Testing, deploying and monitoring all of it

That means software engineering skills matter as much as AI knowledge. Good news if you're a developer already.

## Learn in this order

**1. Programming foundations.** One language properly — Python or JavaScript. Functions, data structures, working with APIs and JSON, Git.

**2. Full-stack basics.** Build and deploy a simple web app with a database. For many learners, the MERN stack is a practical choice.

**3. LLM fundamentals.** Calling an LLM API, prompting, tokens and cost, structured outputs.

**4. Working with your own data.** Embeddings, semantic search and RAG.

**5. Agents and automation.** Tool use, multi-step workflows, guardrails.

**6. Shipping.** Evaluation, Docker, deployment, logging and cost control.

Skipping ahead is tempting — agents are exciting — but the later steps are built entirely on the earlier ones.

## Give every week the same shape

Structure beats motivation. A rhythm that works well:

- **One clear theme per week**, with one small project as the outcome
- **Daily tasks**: learn a concept, build a piece of it, write down what you learned
- **One review at the end of the week**: show what you built, explain it out loud, and get feedback

Explaining your work out loud is underrated. If you can't explain it simply, you haven't learned it yet — and it's exactly the skill interviews test.

## Build proof, not certificates

Certificates show you watched something. Projects show you can **do** something. Aim for a few projects that:

- **Solve a real problem**, even a small one — your college notes, a family business, a community you're part of
- Are **deployed**, with a link someone can actually try
- Have a **clear README**: the problem, how it works, what you'd improve

Three finished, deployed projects are worth more than twenty half-finished tutorials.

## Avoid the common traps

**Tutorial loops** — watching tutorial after tutorial without building on your own. After every tutorial, build something similar *without* it.

**Chasing every new tool** — new frameworks appear weekly. Fundamentals last; most tools don't.

**Learning alone with no feedback** — you can't see your own blind spots. Find a mentor, a study partner or a community, and show your work regularly.

## The most important thing

Consistency beats intensity. A few focused hours every day, for months, with regular feedback, will take you further than any weekend binge. Pick a roadmap, give your week a structure, build in public — and keep going.
